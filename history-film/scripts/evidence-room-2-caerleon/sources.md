Checked 2026-11-19/20 by web search and by reading full texts (Internet Archive scans of the primary works). The Project Gutenberg server was down (HTTP 503), so the primary texts below come from archive.org scans of the same translations.

Claim on trial: "King Arthur's court, and his Round Table, were at Caerleon (the Roman amphitheatre)."

Status key: VERIFIED = text or page read and wording confirmed. REPORTED = a reliable source says it, not confirmed against a second source or the original. MUST VERIFY = check against a print or primary source before it goes in the script. NOT SUPPORTED = the evidence does not back it (or points the other way).

Note on the Cadw booklet: page numbers given for "Trailblazers: Tessa Verney Wheeler" are the printed numbers as read from the PDF text and may be off by one. Check before putting a page number on screen.

Note on OCR: texts marked "(OCR)" were read from machine-read scans. The meaning is reliable; before putting any phrase on screen, check the exact spelling against the page image on archive.org.

## Contradictions to show on screen

1. CADW vs GEOFFREY. Cadw's website and its booklet say Geoffrey wrote that Arthur was crowned at Caerleon and that the amphitheatre was the Round Table's remains. Geoffrey's text has no Round Table and no amphitheatre, and his first coronation of Arthur is at Silchester. The Round Table is Wace's (1155). The Clerk can hold up Geoffrey's page next to Cadw's.
2. SOMEWHAT IMAGINATIVE. Cadw's own page calls Geoffrey "the somewhat imaginative 12th-century scholar". Cadw's own booklet calls the Arthur link "an appealing, (though untrue), story".
3. THE NEWSPAPER DID IT. The Daily Mail paid for the dig in return for "exclusive" Arthurian updates, and the Wheelers "were canny enough not to dispute this romantic idea" (Cadw booklet). Museum Wales (People's Collection): Wheeler linked the site to the legend because it was "likely to attract the considerable funds".
4. AD 90 vs AD 80. Cadw and Museum Wales "Roman Ruins" say the amphitheatre was begun about AD 90. Museum Wales's own blog says the excavators concluded "around AD 80". Say "about AD 90, though one museum page says AD 80".
5. HOW LONG? Museum Wales: "more than 200 years" (blog), "almost 200 years between AD 75 and AD 287" (info sheet), "Around AD 300 the legion left for good" (blog). Say "about two centuries".
6. 1405 FRENCH ARMY. Museum Wales says the French "visited 'King Arthur's Round Table'" at Caerleon. The 1405 chronicler Monstrelet says only "the Round Table, which is a noble abbey"; the Caerleon guess is a footnote query by his 1810 translator. Do not use as the first sighting.
7. WHO DUG IT? Museum Wales and People's Collection credit Mortimer Wheeler. Cadw's booklet says his wife Tessa on site for eight months did "the actual excavation" (quoting historian Lydia Carr). The 1928 report carries both names (R. E. M. Wheeler and T. V. Wheeler).
8. GERALD'S REMAINS. Gerald (1188) walked the ruins and lists palaces, a tower, baths, temples and "theatres". He never says Round Table, and never links the ruins to a table.
9. THE TABLE OF RANK vs THE TABLE OF EQUALS. In Geoffrey the guests sit "according as the rank of each did demand". In Wace the Round Table exists so that no baron can sit higher than another.
10. NINTH BATTLE. Historia Brittonum puts Arthur's ninth battle at "the City of Legion". Giles's 1848 footnote says Exeter; others say Chester or Caerleon. Debated.
11. WHEN DID IT GET ITS NAME? No dated source says. Earliest firm label: Ordnance Survey maps, recorded 1898-1908. A 1587 poem hints at a round table at Caerleon without naming the ruin.
