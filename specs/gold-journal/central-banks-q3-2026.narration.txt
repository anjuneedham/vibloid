Between July and September, the world's central banks bought 321 tonnes of gold. And most of that buying was never announced.

In this entry of The Gold Journal I'm opening the document that counts it, the World Gold Council's third-quarter report, and following three numbers that don't seem to fit together.

The report came out on the third of November. It's called Gold Demand Trends, and the first thing you notice is that it's mostly a table. Rows for jewellery, for bars and coins, for funds, for technology. And one row near the bottom with a long, careful name: central banks and other institutions.

In that row, this quarter says 321 tonnes. A year earlier, the same row said 226. That's a rise of forty-two percent. The average for a single quarter over the last five years is about 227 tonnes, so this was not a normal quarter. It was roughly forty percent above normal.

Add the first three quarters together and you get 675 tonnes so far this year, six percent more than the same stretch of last year. Hold on to that number. We'll come back to what it's up against.

Numbers like 321 tonnes are hard to feel, so I did the arithmetic. Total gold demand in the quarter, counting everything, was 1,369 tonnes, about one percent lower than a year ago. That means central banks were responsible for nearly a quarter of it.

Mines, meanwhile, produced about 1,071 tonnes, a third-quarter record, up three percent. Recycled gold, the old jewellery people sell back, fell eight percent to about 315 tonnes.

Put those side by side. One group was buying far more than it used to. The usual sellers of old gold were holding on to theirs. The grand total barely moved. The mix underneath it moved a lot. That's what a table can show you that a headline can't.

Here's the part that made me stop reading and start writing. Central banks don't have to tell anyone when they buy. Some publish their holdings every month. Many don't. And when you compare what was officially reported with what the quarter's total implies, the gap is large.

According to Kitco's reading of the report, only about 120 of those 321 tonnes were officially reported. That leaves roughly two hundred tonnes, around sixty-three percent, that showed up in the numbers without an announcement. Kitco says that has been the pattern for four years.

Of the buying that was disclosed, the biggest names were China's central bank, at about 63 tonnes, and Poland's, at about 40.

So a headline about central bank buying isn't really about what happened. It's about what was confirmed. The journal's first note: the count always arrives late, and it's never complete.

Now the second number. Jewellery. Demand fell seventeen percent from a year ago, to about 313 tonnes. The Council says that's the weakest third quarter in its data. Bar and coin buying, the thing ordinary savers do, fell nineteen percent, to 268 tonnes, which Kitco calls the lowest quarter in four years. Across all investment, the total slipped fourteen percent.

The Council's explanation for the savers is simple: they waited for a clearer price trend. And it adds, in words Kitco quotes, that fiscal concerns and broader uncertainty have partly offset the higher opportunity cost of holding gold.

Read that again, because it's doing a lot of work. Opportunity cost. Gold pays no interest, so owning it means giving up what that money could earn somewhere else. Ordinary buyers felt that this quarter. Central banks weighed it differently.

The regional split tells the same story from another side. Kitco's summary of the report says bar and coin buying in China rose seven percent, to about 79 tonnes, while India fell forty percent to about 55, and the United States fell forty percent to about 8.

Jewellery in China fell twenty-two percent, to 66 tonnes. In India it fell twenty percent, to about 100.

So the weakness was broad, and the one place where savers kept buying bars and coins was also home to one of the biggest disclosed central bank buyers. I can't tell you why from a table. A table only tells you what happened, and the journal's rule is to say so when the data stops and the guessing starts.

The third number is the price itself. The Council says the London afternoon gold price averaged 4,262 dollars an ounce in the quarter. That's twenty-three percent higher than a year earlier, and the third strongest quarterly average on record. The same tonne of gold cost buyers a great deal more than it did a year ago.

Kitco adds that after a two-month downtrend, the market has since pushed below four thousand dollars. That's the reminder every gold story needs. The record and the retreat sit in the same paragraph. Gold fell here, and it can fall again. Nobody knows where it goes next, not the Council and not me, and this journal won't pretend to.

There's a fourth row worth a look: funds. Gold-backed exchange traded funds lost about 47 tonnes in the second quarter, then added 211 in the third. Kitco says total holdings reached a record, 4,256 tonnes, with Europe and North America doing most of the buying.

So the story isn't that everyone left, and it isn't that everyone arrived. Different groups moved at different speeds. Funds report daily. Central banks report when they choose. Jewellery shoppers don't report at all, they just walk past the counter.

Looking ahead, Kitco says the Council now expects investment demand for the year to rise by about 200 tonnes over last year, down from an earlier estimate of 400. An estimate that gets cut in half is its own kind of note.

So why would a central bank buy gold in the first place? The answer is older than any of these numbers. Gold is one of the few reserve assets that is nobody else's promise. A government bond is a promise from a government. A bank deposit is a promise from a bank. A bar in a vault is just a bar.

Until 1971, the United States still promised other governments it would swap their dollars for gold at a fixed price. When that promise ended, gold stopped being the rulebook of money and became one choice among many for the people who hold reserves.

A central bank also isn't asking what you're asking. It isn't hoping to sell next year at a profit. It holds reserves so that it can be trusted, and so that it has something that works in a crisis no matter who is angry at whom. That is a different job from yours. It has no rent to pay and no deadline.

Here's what I wrote at the bottom of the page.

One. A record in a table is a record, not a recommendation. Central banks bought 321 tonnes. That tells you what they did. It doesn't tell you what you should do.

Two. Buying nobody announced means the news is always behind the buying. By the time a number reaches a headline, it's already history.

Three. Gold has costs. It pays no income, it has to be stored and insured, and dealers charge a spread. It peaked in January 1980 and then took decades to get back to that level in dollar terms. Strong years are not a promise about the next ones.

And four. The most useful thing in this report isn't a number. It's the habit. Open the actual document. Read the row. Ask who is missing from it.

This is not financial advice. It's a note from the journal.
